# Commanding Chaos

A QA & Design case study series. Most QA portfolios show a list of bugs found — I wanted to show the reasoning that gets you there.

"Commanding Chaos" isn't about giving orders in-game. It's about what QA does with a chaotic system: turning a vague player complaint into cause-and-effect a team can actually act on. Every entry follows the same loop — observe a player-facing symptom, form a hypothesis about the system underneath it, classify the risk, and design the validation that would confirm or rule it out.

Each case study pairs a main investigation with a Technical Appendix — systems map, observability requirements, test cases, an extended bug report, a risk checklist, and sources.

## Episodes

**#1 — [Emergent Medieval Battles](./case-studies/chronicles-medieval/commanding-chaos-1.md)**
*Chronicles: Medieval*, Raw Power Games. How do you let a battle feel chaotic without making it feel random? Written entirely from public material, since the game hadn't shipped yet — player-facing causality, delegated agency, formations under pressure, morale as a readable system.

**#2 — [Quest State & NPC Pathing](./case-studies/gothic-remake/commanding-chaos-2.md)**
*Gothic 1 Remake*, Alkimia Interactive. How does an open world stay legible when the player can approach it in almost any order? Three real, documented bug families — arrival-gated dialogue, quest-order dependency, NPC state resets that read as lost progress — cross-checked against community reports, official patch notes, and my own playthrough.
